Janavani Resources · Civic understanding

Know enough to act well.

Public systems become less intimidating when citizens can find the right information, understand the pathway, prepare carefully, and keep the distinction between assistance and authority clear.

Resources are for citizen capacity — not dependency.

Use them to understand a system, check a claim, prepare for action, and continue participating.

02 · Understand

Start with the question, not the form.

Before choosing a document or office, understand what the actual civic problem is. A good resource helps the citizen ask a better question.

01

What happened?

Record the observable facts without assuming the institutional explanation.

02

Who or what is affected?

Identify the person, group, service, place, or public function involved.

03

Which public system is involved?

Determine whether the issue concerns a department, local body, public service, procedure, record, or another civic pathway.

04

What outcome is sought?

Define the practical remedy, clarification, record, service, response, or accountability sought by the citizen.

05

What remains uncertain?

Mark gaps rather than converting assumptions into facts. Uncertainty is useful information when it is made visible.

03 · Prepare

A stronger civic action begins with better preparation.

The exact requirements vary by issue and institution. These are practical preparation categories, not a substitute for the official requirements of a particular authority.

FACTS

Build a factual record.

  • What happened
  • When and where
  • Relevant people or offices
  • What has already been attempted
DOCUMENTS

Collect supporting material.

  • Relevant notices or records
  • Reference numbers
  • Receipts or acknowledgements
  • Other material the official process requires
OBJECTIVE

Define the requested action.

  • Clarification
  • Service or correction
  • Review or response
  • Other lawful civic remedy

04 · Resource discipline

Useful information must remain accountable.

A civic resource is stronger when it tells the citizen what it knows, how it knows it, and where human or official confirmation is still necessary.

01 · SOURCE

Show where it came from.

Prefer traceable official or otherwise reliable sources where the claim depends on them.

02 · STATUS

Show what is verified.

Do not blur an official fact with generated interpretation or citizen-provided information.

03 · LIMIT

Show the boundary.

Explain when a resource cannot determine jurisdiction, legal status, outcome, or institutional responsibility.

04 · NEXT

Show the next responsible step.

When automation cannot safely resolve the question, direct the citizen toward an appropriate official pathway.

05 · Check

Before acting on an answer, ask three questions.

These checks are deliberately simple. Their purpose is to preserve citizen judgment when information is incomplete, changing, or interpreted by technology.

01 · AUTHORITY

Who says this?

Is the information coming from an official source, a structured record, a citizen, an interpretation, or an unknown source?

02 · CONTEXT

Does it fit this situation?

Check location, jurisdiction, date, service, document type, and other conditions that can change the correct pathway.

03 · CONSEQUENCE

What happens if it is wrong?

The higher the consequence, the stronger the need for official confirmation or qualified human review.

06 · Participate

Filing something is not the end of participation.

A complaint, representation, request, or application creates a civic record and a possible pathway for follow-up. Keep the thread intact.

Keep your record.

Save copies of submissions, reference numbers, acknowledgements, dates, responses, and other relevant correspondence.

DATEREFERENCERESPONSEFOLLOW-UP

Read the response.

Distinguish an acknowledgement from a substantive decision, a request for more information, a transfer, a rejection, or another institutional outcome.
